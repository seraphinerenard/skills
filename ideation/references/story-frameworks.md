# Story frameworks for decks and documents

Five narrative spines, when each fits, and how each maps to a slide flow of full-sentence assertion titles. Use this file during Part 2 of the ideation skill, when drafting candidate stories for the user to choose between.

## Why the story comes first

A deck without a spine is a list, and audiences act on arguments, never on lists. The professionals who think hardest about this converge from different directions on the same rule. Barbara Minto (McKinsey's first female consultant, author of *The Pyramid Principle*) built the firm's writing doctrine on stating the governing thought first and arranging everything beneath it as its proof. Nancy Duarte (whose firm built decks for Apple and Al Gore) reverse-engineered great talks in *Resonate*, including Martin Luther King's "I Have a Dream" and Steve Jobs's 2007 iPhone launch, and found the same alternating shape in both. Amazon banned slide decks in favour of six-page narrative memos because, in Bezos's words to shareholders, the narrative structure forces better thought about what matters and how things are connected. The lesson for deck-building: settle the argument before touching layout, because layout cannot rescue an argument that was never made.

## SCQA (Minto)

**Mechanics.** Four beats. **Situation**: a stable fact the audience already accepts, stated in their terms. **Complication**: the thing that broke, changed, or threatens the situation. **Question**: the question the complication forces (usually implicit). **Answer**: your governing thought, one sentence, which the rest of the deck exists to prove. Supporting points beneath the answer follow pyramid rules: each is a full assertion, the set is mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive, and each gets its evidence on its own slide.

**Fits.** Board updates, recommendations to a skeptical or senior audience, any deck where the reader's first question is "why are you telling me this and what do you want".

**Failure mode.** A Situation the audience does not actually accept (the deck dies on slide one, arguing about premises), or an Answer stated so late the audience has already formed their own.

**Slide flow, worked example** (illustrative numbers; the case is an internal proposal to automate client identity checks):

1. Onboarding a new client takes 11 days, and 9 of those days are spent waiting on manual identity checks.
2. Two competitors moved to same-day onboarding this spring, and our trial-to-funded conversion fell from 61% to 44% over the same period.
3. Automated verification cuts onboarding below one day for roughly 85% of applicants at about $3.10 per check.
4. Three vendors clear our compliance bar, and their pricing differs mainly on how much manual review they still require.
5. A phased rollout that starts with low-risk retail accounts reaches 80% automation by March without touching institutional flows.
6. The build costs $140K, pays back in about seven months, and the main risk is reviewer staffing through the cutover.

Read the titles alone: the argument is complete. That is the standard every storyline must meet.

## ABT: And, But, Therefore (Olson)

**Mechanics.** Randy Olson (marine biologist turned filmmaker, *Houston, We Have a Narrative*) compressed narrative into one sentence shape: "[context] AND [more context], BUT [the problem], THEREFORE [the resolution]". He traces the form to USC screenwriting teacher Frank Daniel and to the *South Park* creators' "but/therefore rule": if the beats of a story connect with "and then", the story is dead; they must connect with "but" and "therefore". The power is in the single But: one problem, sharply stated. Olson's diagnostic for bad decks is "And, And, And": facts accumulating with no tension.

**Fits.** Pitches, one-decision memos, short decks (under ten slides), any audience with limited patience. The tightest of the spines.

**Failure mode.** Multiple Buts (the story fragments), or a Therefore that does not actually follow from the But.

**Slide flow, same case:**

1. Our funnel delivers about 1,200 qualified applicants a month, and each funded client is worth roughly $312 a year.
2. Manual identity checks hold every applicant for nine days, and 38% abandon before funding.
3. Abandonment during the identity wait costs an estimated $1.7M a year in lost annual revenue.
4. Automating verification removes the wait for 85% of applicants, and the remaining 15% get human review within a day.
5. A $140K build recovers most of the $1.7M, so we should fund the pilot this quarter and measure abandonment weekly.

## Duarte's sparkline

**Mechanics.** Alternate between **what is** (the audience's flawed present, described honestly) and **what could be** (the concrete better state), widening the gap each cycle, and end on the **new bliss**: the world after adoption. The alternation creates the tension that a monotone description never does. Duarte's sparkline analyses show King's speech and Jobs's iPhone launch both riding this alternation for their full length, and her casting rule keeps the presenter honest: the audience is the hero, and the presenter is the mentor, "Yoda guiding Luke Skywalker".

**Fits.** Persuasion against inertia: vision decks, change-management, sales to a comfortable incumbent customer, conference talks. Use when resistance is emotional rather than analytical.

**Failure mode.** "What could be" stated as adjectives ("a seamless future") rather than as scenes with nouns and numbers, or a new bliss the presenter cannot actually deliver.

**Slide flow, same case:**

1. Today a new client waits 11 days while three teams pass the same PDFs around by email.
2. A client who signs at 9 a.m. could be funded by lunch, with the compliance evidence archived before anyone reads email.
3. Right now our reviewers spend most of the week re-keying data the applicant already typed once.
4. Reviewers could spend that week on the 15% of cases that genuinely need judgment, which is the work they were hired for.
5. Same-day onboarding becomes the reason clients choose us, and the audit trail gets stronger rather than weaker.

## Monroe's motivated sequence

**Mechanics.** Alan Monroe (Purdue, 1930s) structured persuasive speech in five steps: **Attention** (the hook, in the audience's world), **Need** (the problem, with evidence, until it feels urgent), **Satisfaction** (your solution, in enough detail to be believed), **Visualization** (the world with the solution working, and the world without it), **Action** (exactly what to approve, sign, or fund, by when).

**Fits.** Proposals and RFP responses, funding asks, safety and compliance cases. The five steps map almost one-to-one onto what an RFP evaluator needs to score: understanding, solution, evidence, plan, ask. Part 3 of the ideation skill builds on this spine.

**Failure mode.** A vague Action step. The last slide must name the decision, the amount, the date, and the first milestone, or the sequence was wasted.

## The story spine (Kenn Adams, via Pixar)

**Mechanics.** Kenn Adams built the spine as an improv exercise in 1991; it reached Pixar through improv training and spread widely when story artist Emma Coats included it in her 2012 "22 rules of storytelling". The template, verbatim: "Once upon a time there was ___. Every day, ___. One day ___. Because of that, ___. Because of that, ___. Until finally ___." (Many versions append "And ever since then…".) A chain of consequence built for narrative case studies.

**Fits.** Teaching decks, incident post-mortems, case-study sections inside a larger deck. The "because of that" chain forces causality where most decks settle for chronology.

**Failure mode.** Using it for a recommendation. It explains how things happened; it does not argue what to do.

## The horizontal flow test and storylining

Consulting firms storyline decks as "dot-dash" outlines before any slide is built: each dot is a slide title written as a complete assertion, each dash beneath it is the evidence that slide will carry. Two tests before building:

1. **Horizontal flow.** Read the titles alone, in order. They must deliver the entire argument with no gaps, no repeats, and no title that merely names a topic. "Vendor comparison" fails; "Three vendors clear our compliance bar, and pricing differs mainly on manual-review volume" passes.
2. **Vertical anchoring.** For each slide, the evidence beneath the title must prove that title and nothing else. Evidence that proves a different sentence belongs on a different slide, and a title no evidence can prove gets rewritten or cut.

## Choosing a spine

| The deck's job | Spine |
|---|---|
| Recommend a decision to seniors or a board | SCQA |
| Win one decision fast, few slides | ABT |
| Move an audience that is comfortable where it is | Sparkline |
| Proposal or RFP response with an explicit ask | Monroe |
| Teach what happened and why | Story spine |

Draft the two or three candidates from different rows, write each as a five-to-eight-sentence synopsis plus its title flow, and put them in front of the user with AskUserQuestion. The user picks the story; the storyline then gets built and approved before a line of layout code is written.
