## 1. THE DATASET AND THE TWO TRACKS
Source for all of this section: accuracy results preprint, https://statmodeling.stat.columbia.edu/wp-content/uploads/2021/10/M5_accuracy_competition.pdf (Table 1, sections 2–3), access 2026-07-12.

- Data owner: Walmart, described as the world's largest retailer by revenue. 3 US states: California (CA), Texas (TX), Wisconsin (WI); 10 stores; 3 product categories (Hobbies, Foods, Household); 7 departments; 3,049 distinct products.
- 42,840 series arise from a 12-level hierarchy. Exact per-level counts (Table 1): Level 1 Total = 1; Level 2 State = 3; Level 3 Store = 10; Level 4 Category = 3; Level 5 Department = 7; Level 6 State-Category = 9; Level 7 State-Department = 21; Level 8 Store-Category = 30; Level 9 Store-Department = 70; Level 10 Product (aggregated over all stores) = 3,049; Level 11 Product-State = 9,147; Level 12 Product-Store = 30,490. Sum = 42,840. The bottom level 12 (30,490 = 3,049 × 10) is the only level competitors forecast directly; all higher levels are summed up from it (coherent/bottom-up aggregation).
- Exogenous data: calendar with special events/holidays; SNAP (Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program) — a binary 0/1 flag per state indicating days when SNAP EBT purchases are allowed (roughly 10 days/month per state); weekly selling prices (sell_prices), which vary over time and drive price/promotion features. Series display heavy intermittency (many zeros).
- Timeline: competition opened 2020-03-03 (train set released), ended 2020-06-30; team-merger deadline 2020-06-23; validation phase 2020-03-03 to 2020-05-31 with a live public leaderboard (max 5 submissions/day); test/private phase from 2020-06-01 with no feedback. Train = days 1–1913; the validation-phase target was days 1914–1941 (2016-04-25 to 2016-05-22); the final private test window was the following 28 days (1942–1969). Forecast horizon h = 28 days.
- Prizes: total pool US$100,000 split evenly between the two tracks (US$50,000 each). Accuracy ladder: 1st $25,000, 2nd $10,000, 3rd $5,000, 4th $3,000, 5th $2,000, plus a $5,000 best-student prize. Reproducibility was mandatory (code within 14 days, ≥98% WRMSSE reproducibility). Awards given at the virtual M5 conference 2020-10-29.
- Two parallel tracks, same 42,840 series: M5 Accuracy = point forecasts scored by WRMSSE; M5 Uncertainty = probabilistic forecasts of 9 quantiles scored by WSPL (Weighted Scaled Pinball Loss). A team could enter and win in both.

## 2. THE EVALUATION METRICS (exact definitions)

WRMSSE (Accuracy track). Source: accuracy preprint, eqs. around lines 437–500.
- Per-series RMSSE = sqrt( [ (1/h) Σ_{t=n+1}^{n+h} (y_t − ŷ_t)^2 ] / [ (1/(n−1)) Σ_{t=2}^{n} (y_t − y_{t−1})^2 ] ). The denominator is the in-sample one-step-ahead MSE of the Naive (random-walk) forecast, computed only over periods after the product's first non-zero sale (products enter the catalogue at different dates).
- WRMSSE = Σ_{i=1}^{42,840} w_i · RMSSE_i. Weights w_i are the series' cumulative actual dollar sales (units × price) over the last 28 days of the training sample. Because dollar sales are additive up the hierarchy, all 12 levels receive equal total weight (each level's series-weights sum to 1); the overall WRMSSE effectively averages 12 equally-weighted levels. Lower is better. Squared error is deliberate: absolute error is minimized by the median and, on zero-heavy intermittent series, would reward forecasts collapsing toward zero; squared error targets the mean, which is the quantity of business interest.

SPL / WSPL (Uncertainty track). Sources: Kaggle evaluation page https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-uncertainty ; metric derivation blog https://michelbaudin.com/2021/07/16/evaluating-sales-forecasts/ ; uncertainty results paper metadata.
- Nine quantiles required: u ∈ {0.005, 0.025, 0.165, 0.250, 0.500, 0.750, 0.835, 0.975, 0.995}. These are the median plus the lower/upper bounds of the central 50% (0.25/0.75), 67% (0.165/0.835), 95% (0.025/0.975), and 99% (0.005/0.995) prediction intervals. (Caution: some secondary summaries loosely call these "50/66/75/95/99" — the precise interval mapping is 50/67/95/99. Flagging this as a common secondary-source imprecision.)
- Pinball (quantile) loss at quantile u for actual y and predicted quantile Q(u): if y ≥ Q(u), loss = (y − Q(u))·u; if y < Q(u), loss = (Q(u) − y)·(1 − u). It is the asymmetric V-shaped loss whose minimizer is the true u-quantile.
- SPL for one series at one quantile = [ (1/h) Σ_{t} pinball_u(y_t, Q_t(u)) ] / [ (1/(n−1)) Σ_{t=2}^{n} |y_t − y_{t−1}| ]. The denominator is the in-sample MAE of the Naive forecast (absolute-error scaling), analogous to RMSSE's scaling but with absolute values.
- Per-series score = the SPL averaged with equal weights across the 9 quantiles. WSPL = Σ_i w_i · (mean-SPL_i), using the SAME dollar-sales weights w_i and the same 12-level equal-weighting as WRMSSE. Lower is better.
- Metric critique to flag: on the michelbaudin thread, practitioner Stefan de Kok argues WSPL is highly "gameable" — asymmetric rounding of quantile bounds (round down below the median, round up above) can improve the score without improving genuine calibration. This is consistent with the fact that the 2nd-place uncertainty team (GoodsForecast) explicitly used "rounding heuristics" to improve the target metric.

## 3. WHAT WON — ACCURACY TRACK
Participation (accuracy preprint §3.6): 7,092 participants on 5,507 teams from 101 countries; 88,136 submissions. Benchmark-beating: 2,666 teams (48.4%) beat Naive, 1,972 (35.8%) beat seasonal-Naive, and only 415 (7.5%) beat the strongest statistical benchmark ES_bu (bottom-up exponential smoothing). Of those 415, five beat ES_bu by >20%, 42 by >15%, 106 by >10%, 249 by >5%. Only the top five teams exceeded 20%. Headline result: the top ML method was 22.4% more accurate than the best statistical benchmark; per the paper, LightGBM/ML dominated and every top-50 method used cross-learning.

Top-5 teams (WRMSSE and improvement over ES_bu; Table 3):
1. YJ_STU = YeonJun Im, a senior undergraduate at Kyung Hee University, South Korea. WRMSSE 0.520, +22.4%. Method: equal-weighted arithmetic mean of LightGBM models trained by pooling data per store (10 models), per store-category (30), and per store-department (70); two variants each (recursive and non-recursive), giving 220 models total, each series forecast by averaging 6 of them (different learners/train sets). Objective: Tweedie (maximizing Tweedie negative log-likelihood), no early stopping. Features: identifiers, calendar/special events, promotions/SNAP, prices, and lagged unit sales in both recursive and non-recursive form. CV over the last four 28-day windows, selecting on both mean AND standard deviation of error (recursive models were more accurate but less stable, so he combined recursive+non-recursive for robustness). His own paper: "Simple averaging of direct and recursive forecasts via partial pooling using machine learning." Confirmation of the win and undergraduate status: https://x.com/spyrosmakrid/status/1280126158105915392
2. Matthias = Matthias Anderer. WRMSSE 0.528, +21.3%. Equal-weighted LightGBM (10 models trained per store) externally adjusted by multipliers derived from N-BEATS forecasts at the top 5 aggregation levels to capture trend; 5 multipliers → 50 models; custom asymmetric loss; LightGBM used only calendar/price features (no lagged sales), while N-BEATS used only historical sales. Repo (documents the loss-multiplier ensemble values {0.9, 0.93, 0.95, 0.97, 0.99} and a top-down alignment step): https://github.com/matthiasanderer/m5-accuracy-competition
3. mf = Yunho Jeon & Sihyeon Seong. WRMSSE 0.536, +20.2%. Equal-weighted combination of 43 deep-learning nets (DeepAR-style, multiple LSTM layers, recursive), 24 with dropout + 19 without, originating from 12 models (best training instances by CV). Also used Tweedie regression (weights optimized on sampled predictions), Adam + cosine-annealing LR, ~100 features similar to the winner's.
4. monsaraida = Masanori Miyahara. WRMSSE 0.536, +20.1%. Non-recursive LightGBM, 10 models per store, but each of the 4 horizon-weeks forecast by a separate model → 40 models; Tweedie, no early stopping, no hyperparameter tuning; price features found highly important.
5. Alan Lahoud. WRMSSE 0.536, +20.1%. Recursive LightGBM, 7 models per department; Poisson regression with early stopping; validated on a random 500-day sample; then externally adjusted via multipliers so each store-department series' mean matched the prior 28 days.

Notable others: N60610 (16th) blended LightGBM + Kalman filter, selecting per series; MonashSL_STU (17th) LightGBM + Pooled Regression Model; Nodalpoints (21st) LightGBM + NNs; Astral (36th) Prophet-like model mixing XGBoost/LightGBM. Vs the intermittent-demand standard Croston (CRO): the winner beat CRO by 45.6% on average (77.9% at level 1 falling to 4.5% at level 12) — CRO retained value only at the lowest, product-store level. sNaive beat CRO by 11.5% overall but LOST to CRO at levels 10/11/12 (by 13.0%/20.2%/27.0%), confirming Croston's niche on sparse bottom-level series.

## 4. WHAT WON — UNCERTAINTY TRACK
Participation (uncertainty results paper via secondary summaries): 1,137 participants on 892 teams from 94 countries; 9,936 submissions (~1/5 the teams and ~1/10 the submissions of the Accuracy track). Six benchmarks: Naive, seasonal-Naive, exponential smoothing (ETS), simple exponential smoothing, ARIMA, and a non-parametric Kernel/empirical quantile estimator; ARIMA was the strongest benchmark. Benchmark-beating: 202 teams (~22.6%) beat ARIMA, 10 beat it by >20%, and the winner beat ARIMA by 24.6%. (Conflict flag: one secondary summary phrases this as "202 out of 894 teams"; the organizer participation figure is 892 teams — a 2-team discrepancy in secondary retellings; treat 892 as the reported team count.) Improvement over ARIMA was steeply level-dependent: ~44% at level 1, ~26% at levels 2–7, ~16% at levels 8–9, dropping to ~2% at levels 10/11/12 — probabilistic gains, like point gains, collapse at the sparse bottom levels and in the extreme tail quantiles.

Winner: team "Everyday Low SPLices" = A. David Lander & Russ Wolfinger (SAS/JMP). Approach: a separate LightGBM quantile model per aggregation level × quantile, ~126 models in total. Handcrafted features: rolling average, rolling median, day-of-week, skewness, kurtosis, quantiles of past sales, and more. Key trick: a sampling-and-augmentation scheme — sub-sampling subsets of series and adding Gaussian noise to both features and targets — to make the trees robust; plus careful tuning and CV. Their solution paper: "Forecasting with gradient boosted trees: augmentation, tuning, and cross-validation strategies" (IJF 2022), https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0169207021002090

2nd place: GoodsForecast (Nikolay Mamonov, Evgeny Golubyatnikov, Daniel Kanevskiy, Igor Gusakov). Empirical distribution built by judgmentally combining three heterogeneous estimators at different aggregation levels: (i) histogram of past observations, (ii) trend via singular spectrum analysis, (iii) an ML model for external factors — then quantiles refined with rounding heuristics that improved the WSPL. Paper: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0169207022000541 (IJF 38(4): 1434–1441).

Other documented entrants (rank-flag): the "Hybrid gradient boosting and autoregressive recurrent neural network for quantile estimation" paper (LightGBM + DeepAR hybrid, Ernest Chiew & Shin Siang Choong, IJF 38(4): 1442–1447) is a 13th-place solution, NOT 3rd — it won on the MCB average-rank test despite ranking 13th on WSPL. https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/intfor/v38y2022i4p1442-1447.html . The exact 3rd-place uncertainty team name could NOT be verified from open sources — flag as unverified. General finding: top uncertainty methods used gradient boosting for direct quantile prediction; parametric count distributions (Poisson, negative-binomial, generalized-Poisson) beat ARIMA by ~8% with the biggest gains in the central 50%/75% region, while the non-parametric Kernel benchmark actually underperformed ETS/ARIMA.

## 5. GLOBAL vs LOCAL — WHY CROSS-LEARNING WON
Every top-50 accuracy method used cross-learning (one global model trained across many series) rather than one model per series. The organizers' Finding 3 states that because M5 series are aligned, highly correlated, and hierarchical, cross-learning was far easier to exploit than in M4, achieving superior accuracy over series-by-series fitting while reducing compute and mitigating short-history problems (accuracy preprint, Findings section).

The theoretical backing is Montero-Manso & Hyndman, arXiv:2008.00444 / IJF 37(4): 1632–1653 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.00444). Their central, counter-intuitive claim: "global methods are not more restrictive than local methods; both can produce the same forecasts without any assumptions about similarity of the series." A single global function fitted to all series can replicate any set of per-series local functions. The decisive asymmetry is complexity: the parameter count of the local approach grows with the number of series, while a global model's stays constant, so a global model can afford a much more complex/expressive function (deep nets, high-order autoregression, long memory) at the same total data/parameter budget and still generalize. Empirically they show global linear autoregressions match local methods with up to two orders of magnitude fewer parameters. This is the mechanism behind LightGBM's M5 dominance: one global GBM sees all ~30,490 bottom series at once, learns cross-series structure and price/calendar effects, and out-generalizes per-series ARIMA/ETS/Croston. (Caveat from a secondary practitioner note: cross-learning's benefit is uneven — it mainly helps items with more non-zero history; on the sparsest series Croston-type local methods still hold value, matching the organizers' level-12 findings.)

## 6. OBJECTIVES/LOSSES, FEATURES, AND THE "MAGIC MULTIPLIER"

Loss/objective choices.
- Tweedie loss dominated the Accuracy track (winner, 3rd, 4th all used it; 5th used Poisson). Tweedie (compound Poisson-Gamma, power p typically 1.1–1.2) fits intermittent retail demand because it places a genuine probability mass at exactly zero while modelling a continuous, non-negative, right-skewed positive part — the exact shape of zero-inflated, sporadic unit sales. It also optimizes toward the conditional mean, which is what WRMSSE's squared error rewards. Source for the winners' use of Tweedie and the "probability mass of zero, non-negative highly right-skewed" rationale: accuracy preprint (winner description). A practitioner note (Christophe Nicault, https://www.christophenicault.com/post/m5_forecasting_accuracy/) confirms Tweedie power 1.1/1.2 and that competitors could not directly optimize WRMSSE, so they trained on RMSE/Tweedie and monitored WRMSSE separately.
- Quantile/pinball loss was the natural objective for the Uncertainty track — GBMs trained one model per quantile with pinball loss to predict each of the 9 quantiles directly (Lander & Wolfinger's 126-model grid).

Feature engineering (common across top solutions; sources: accuracy preprint winner descriptions; Analytics Vidhya M5 summary https://medium.com/analytics-vidhya/predicting-the-future-with-learnings-from-the-m5-competition-d54e84ca3d0d ; Nicault blog): sales lags, rolling means/medians/std over multiple windows, calendar features (day-of-week, month, events/holidays), SNAP flags, and price features (current price, price momentum, price relative to history) which several top teams (e.g., monsaraida) reported as among the most important. Recursive multi-step approaches recompute lag features from prior forecasts (compounding error over 28 days); direct/non-recursive approaches train a distinct model per horizon step or per horizon-week (monsaraida's 4-models-per-store). The winner deliberately ensembled both because recursive was more accurate but less stable.

The "magic multiplier" / loss-scaling.
Two related things carry this name, and both are genuine M5 lessons:
1. Principled reconciliation multipliers used by winners: Matthias Anderer (2nd) multiplied his bottom-level LightGBM forecasts by trend factors derived from N-BEATS at the top 5 levels, and ensembled across loss-scaling values {0.9, 0.93, 0.95, 0.97, 0.99} (his repo's LOSS_MULTIPLIER); Alan Lahoud (5th) multiplied to force each store-department mean to match the prior 28 days. The organizers' Finding 5 ("beneficial effect of external adjustments") credits these multipliers with reducing bias and aligning bottom-level forecasts to trends visible only at higher aggregation levels.
2. The community "magic multiplier" and the public/private shake-up: many teams simply multiplied all forecasts by a constant to lift the public-leaderboard WRMSSE. This worked because, in the five prior years, June sales rose relative to May, so an upward-scaled (optimistic) forecast scored well on the validation window; the private test window did not repeat that pattern, so multiplier-tuned models overfit the public LB and dropped sharply — a large leaderboard shake-up. Source: Nicault, https://www.christophenicault.com/post/m5_forecasting_accuracy/ . Lesson: robust CV over multiple 28-day windows (what the top teams did) beat public-LB chasing; the winner explicitly selected on error stability, not just mean error.

Organizer "key findings" (accuracy paper, section 4.3), quoted-substance: Finding 1 — M5 was the first M competition where ALL top methods were pure ML and beat every statistical benchmark and their combinations (contrast M4, whose winners were ML-statistical hybrids). Finding 2 — value of combining: winner = equal-weight of 6 models, 2nd = 5 models, 3rd = 43 nets; simple equal weighting beat weighted schemes in nearly all cases. Finding 3 — value of cross-learning. Finding 4 — top methods beat the 24 benchmarks by >20% in WRMSSE. Finding 5 — external multiplier adjustments helped. Finding 6 — effective CV (≥4 rolling 28-day windows) was decisive; many teams failed to pick their own best submission. Finding 7 — exogenous variables mattered: ESX (ES with promo/event regressors) beat plain ES_td by 6%, ARIMAX beat ARIMA_td by 13%, though seasonality captured at higher levels was the single biggest driver. The Conclusions paper (Makridakis, Petropoulos, Spiliotis 2022) generalizes: complex global ML methods gave large accuracy and uncertainty gains over statistical benchmarks, but the gains shrank at lower hierarchical levels and in the distribution tails, and global ML is the appropriate default for retail-scale hierarchical demand.
