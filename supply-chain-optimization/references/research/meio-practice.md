# MEIO in industry, 2020–2026: consulting fact sheet

## 1. Guaranteed-service (GSM) vs stochastic-service (SSM): which framing the software uses

- Two competing academic streams solve the same safety-stock-placement problem under different assumptions about how a stage responds to demand it cannot cover from stock. SSM traces to Clark and Scarf (1960); GSM traces to Simpson (1958), then generalized to networks by Graves and Willems (2000). Note the origin correction: the GSM base model is Simpson 1958, not Graves-Willems, who extended it to spanning-tree networks. Source: Eruguz et al. 2016 survey abstract, https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/proeco/v172y2016icp110-125.html
- The GSM is the framing that commercial MEIO software overwhelmingly implements. The lineage is direct: Sean Willems co-founded Optiant in 2000 to commercialize the Graves-Willems GSM (Optiant PowerChain Suite), and Optiant was acquired by Logility in March 2010 for $3.3M cash. Source: https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/optiants-co-founder-sean-willems-named-2010-provider-pro-to-know-85201677.html and https://www.supplychainbrain.com/articles/7493-logility-acquires-optiant-inventory-optimization-vendor
- The commercial MEIO software category was pioneered by SmartOps (acquired by SAP, 2013), Optiant (acquired by Logility, 2010), and LogicTools (acquired by IBM). Source, search summary citing the "typology and literature review on stochastic multi-echelon inventory models" (Eur. J. Oper. Res.), https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0377221718301802
- Direct primary confirmation of GSM in current software: Optilogic's Cyclo engine documentation states verbatim, "Cyclo uses a Guaranteed Service Model (GSM) approach to optimize service-time relationships between facilities and derive recommended safety stock levels." Source: https://optilogic.com/resources/help-center/docs/getting-started-with-cyclo-multi-echelon-inventory-optimization
- The flagship GSM industrial deployment is Hewlett-Packard. The Interfaces paper "Accelerating the Profitability of Hewlett-Packard's Supply Chains" (Interfaces 34(1), 2004, pp. 59–72) reports realized savings from the first two projects (Digital Camera and Inkjet Supplies businesses) "exceeding $130 million," using the guaranteed-service network approach. Source: https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/inte.0103.0054 and https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/orinte/v34y2004i1p59-72.html
- GSM's headline limitation, load-bearing for section 4: the Graves-Willems (2000) GSM "requires the bill of materials (BOM) to be a spanning tree with bounded demands and deterministic lead times, which are difficult to satisfy for complex production systems." Source, search summary of SAP Community / arXiv 2306.10961 discussion, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.10961
- Evidence GSM is not merely the easier model: De Smet et al. (2019), "Optimising installation (R,Q) policies in distribution networks with stochastic lead times: a comparative analysis of guaranteed- and stochastic service models," Int. J. Prod. Res. 57(13), pp. 4148–4165, finds GSM outperforms SSM in over 80% of simulated instances with an average total-cost improvement near 10%, overturning earlier work (Graves and Willems 2003; Simchi-Levi and Zhao 2012) that reported a small gap. Source: https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/00207543.2018.1518606

## 2. Commercial MEIO software, by modelling approach

Analytic (GSM/closed-form) vs simulation-optimization vs probabilistic-stochastic, with the public technical detail that could be verified:

- **ToolsGroup SO99+** — probabilistic / stochastic, not GSM. Public description: "SO99+ is a stochastic solution... uses order-line details to generate forecast with confidence interval... The modelling is combinatoric and not assumed to be normal distribution." Its proprietary "Stock-to-Service" curves map service level to safety stock per item-location, and MEIO balances across echelons, locations, and BOM levels. Sources: https://www.toolsgroup.com/product/so99/ and https://www.toolsgroup.com/blog/multi-echelon-inventory-optimization-toolsgroup-guide/
- **Coupa / LLamasoft Supply Chain Guru (Inventory Guru)** — demand-classification-driven analytic optimization inside a platform that also has a discrete-event simulation engine. Coupa acquired LLamasoft in November 2020. The engine segments demand into "smooth, erratic, slow and lumpy" classes, calculates optimal safety stocks with risk-pooling trade-offs, and the 2013 "breakthrough" was branded Adaptive Intelligent Inventory Optimization (AI+IO). Supply Chain Guru couples inventory optimization, network design, and simulation ("digital twin") in one tool. Sources: https://www.coupa.com/products/supply-chain-design/ and https://www.sdcexec.com/warehousing/news/11185315/llamasoft-inc-breakthrough-in-multiechelon-inventory-optimization (the older help.llama.ai safety-stock page returns HTTP 403 to automated retrieval)
- **Blue Yonder (Luminate / Inventory Optimization)** — publicly described as risk-pooling MEIO that decides both how much to hold and where, using AI/ML. Blue Yonder does not disclose in public docs whether the core is GSM or SSM, and the systems-integrator alliance pages that resell it repeat the risk-pooling framing without adding formulation detail. Treat the underlying formulation as undisclosed. Source: https://info.blueyonder.com/blue-yonder-network/what-is-multi-echelon-inventory-management
- **SAP IBP for Inventory (formerly SmartOps EIS-LO)** — proprietary; public framing is contradictory, so flag it. SAP acquired SmartOps in 2013 and rebranded it "SAP Enterprise Inventory and Service-Level Optimization," which became part of IBP. One SAP-community description calls it "a stochastic approach to determine the appropriate balance between inventory investment and service level at every node," and states "the algorithm behind single/multi stage inventory optimization is proprietary and classified code." Separate secondary write-ups instead describe IBP MEIO as "based on the Guaranteed Service method." The exact GSM-vs-SSM classification is not publicly confirmable. Sources: https://community.sap.com/t5/supply-chain-management-blog-posts-by-sap/sap-enterprise-inventory-and-service-level-optimization-formerly-smartops/ba-p/13063079 and https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/sap-acquires-smartops-to-help-customers-optimize-inventory-and-service-levels-in-supply-chain-192609031.html
- **Optilogic Cosmic Frog — Cyclo (MEIO) and Dendro (simulation-optimization)** — the clearest public split. Cyclo is explicitly GSM: it optimizes service-time commitments (Incoming Service Time SI, Outgoing Service Time S) between facilities, computes Net Replenishment Time (NRT = SI + fixed lead times − S), and translates service-time decisions into safety-stock requirements. Its sibling Dendro "operationalizes those policies through ongoing simulation-optimization." Both run in the same Cosmic Frog environment alongside network optimization. Source: https://optilogic.com/resources/help-center/docs/getting-started-with-cyclo-multi-echelon-inventory-optimization
- **GAINS (GAINSystems)** — probabilistic MEIO. Public description: "Probabilistic distributions are incorporated for variables such as demand, lead times, and supply," combined with ML/heuristics/AI-driven optimization, and paired with probabilistic forecasting and short-cycle planning. Sources: https://gainsystems.com/platform/meio/ and https://gainsystems.com/blog/a-complete-guide-to-meio/
- **Kinaxis (Maestro / RapidResponse)** — offers both single-echelon (SEIP) and multi-echelon (MEIO) planning natively, and separately markets a partner engine, "Probabilistic Multi-Echelon Inventory Optimization (MEIO) by Wahupa," which "quantifies the uncertainty." The Wahupa engine is the explicitly stochastic/probabilistic option. Sources: https://www.kinaxis.com/en/solutions/applications/probabilistic-meio-wahupa and https://www.kinaxis.com/sites/default/files/resources/inventory-planning-and-optimization-brochure-kinaxis_0.pdf

Net pattern: the "network design" and safety-stock-placement tools (Optilogic Cyclo, and the Optiant/SmartOps lineage) sit on GSM; the "planning/forecasting" vendors (ToolsGroup, GAINS, Kinaxis-Wahupa) foreground probabilistic-stochastic engines. Several vendors keep the exact formulation proprietary.

## 3. What teams actually deploy: single-echelon vs true MEIO

- Adoption is low relative to the marketing. A practitioner/vendor figure repeatedly cited: "Only 13% of companies adopted multi-echelon approaches," attributed to lack of advanced modelling tools, "black box" implementations, and thin integrator expertise to defend counterintuitive recommendations. Source (Arkieva): https://blog.arkieva.com/multi-echelon-inventory-optimization-challenges-solutions/
- A competing, higher estimate circulates in vendor content: "Adoption of MEIO in planning workflows currently sits at 30–40%, with 60–70% of enterprises targeting deployment by 2027." This is a Sophus (vendor) blog number with no underlying survey disclosed. Source: https://sophus.ai/multi-echelon-inventory-optimization-guide/
- Caveat on both numbers: neither the 13% nor the 30–40% figure traces to a named, methodologically disclosed survey in the public record. Treat them as vendor/analyst-blog estimates, not audited statistics.
- The structural reason single-echelon persists: "legacy ERP and planning systems were typically built for single-location logic and simply lack the capability to perform network-wide optimization." ERP/APS safety-stock is set node-by-node, ignoring the service-and-cost interdependency between customer and supplier stages, which the MEIO literature flags as producing poor service or non-cost-optimal stock. Sources: https://sophus.ai/multi-echelon-inventory-optimization-guide/ and https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0925527315005162
- Analyst context on the vendor field: the 2024 Gartner Magic Quadrant for Supply Chain Planning Solutions (published April 23, 2024) placed Kinaxis, Blue Yonder, o9, SAP, and Oracle among Leaders, and named 20 vendors including Coupa, GAINSystems, Logility, ToolsGroup, RELEX, and Slimstock. Gartner does not publish a standalone MEIO adoption rate in the public excerpts. Source: https://www.gartner.com/en/documents/5374263 and the ToolsGroup recognition post https://www.toolsgroup.com/news/toolsgroup-recognized-in-the-2024-gartner-magic-quadrant-for-supply-chain-planning-solutions/
- A widely cited benefit figure attributed to Gartner (2016): MEIO "reduce[d] their inventories up to 30% and improve[d] item availability up to 5%." This appears in the Achkar et al. (2024) EJOR paper citing Gartner 2016. Source: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.10961

## 4. Known failure modes reported by practitioners

- Master data quality is the first gate. A named success factor is "establishing a single source of truth for item master data, historical demand, lead times, bill of materials (BOM), and holding costs," which practitioners report as a common implementation blocker; fragmented and legacy master data "slows adoption and limits model accuracy." Sources: https://www.ptc.com/en/technologies/service-lifecycle-management/service-parts-management/multi-echelon-inventory-optimization and https://sophus.ai/multi-echelon-inventory-optimization-guide/
- GSM's assumptions break on real BOMs and lead times. The classic Graves-Willems model needs a spanning-tree BOM, bounded demand, and deterministic lead times; complex production networks violate all three, which is why the 2020–2026 research effort (Achkar et al., De Smet et al., Eruguz et al.) is largely about relaxing them. Source: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.10961
- Normal-distribution demand assumptions are the loudest practitioner critique. Lokad argues the safety-stock model's "assumption of a normal distribution for both demand and lead times is unrealistic," gives the concrete failure (lead times short except during supplier stockouts, when they jump), and argues that no vendor can deliver "best" safety stocks if the underlying logic is flawed. Sources: https://www.lokad.com/tv/2019/1/9/why-safety-stock-is-unsafe/ and https://www.lokad.com/inventory-optimization/
- Intermittent / lumpy demand defeats standard models. Service-parts demand "is often intermittent and unpredictable," and MEIO "must model demand variability realistically to avoid over- or under-stocking." ToolsGroup and LLamasoft market non-normal demand handling precisely because normal-based safety stock mis-sizes the long tail. Sources: https://www.ptc.com/en/technologies/service-lifecycle-management/service-parts-management/multi-echelon-inventory-optimization and https://www.toolsgroup.com/blog/multi-echelon-inventory-optimization-toolsgroup-guide/
- Projects stall on distrust of counterintuitive output and on misaligned incentives, not only on math. The Arkieva practitioner account is specific: MEIO often pushes safety stock downstream or increases a regional DC's stock while cutting the network total, so a DC manager on a local reduction target resists a recommendation to add days of cover ("3 to 5 days; that's a 66% increase") even when the CFO backs a 30% network reduction; supplier-retailer stocking shifts stall when the benefit lands on one party's balance sheet; and "even if people intellectually agreed, they wanted to see it in practice first before they started believing it." Source: https://blog.arkieva.com/multi-echelon-inventory-optimization-challenges-solutions/
- Organizational silos and disconnected systems limit optimization, because MEIO depends on cross-functional, network-wide information flow. Source: https://www.ptc.com/en/technologies/service-lifecycle-management/service-parts-management/multi-echelon-inventory-optimization

## 5. The simulation alternative

When analytic MEIO assumptions (deterministic lead times, tractable distributions, tree BOMs) fail, firms simulate the network to set stock, either discrete-event or Monte Carlo, usually wrapped in an optimizer.

- Same-vendor split as proof of the pattern: Optilogic pairs the GSM analytic engine (Cyclo) with a simulation-optimization engine (Dendro) that "operationalizes those policies through ongoing simulation-optimization" in the same model. Source: https://optilogic.com/resources/help-center/docs/getting-started-with-cyclo-multi-echelon-inventory-optimization
- Named public case study, semiconductors: Morrice and Valdez, "Discrete Event Simulation in Supply Chain Planning and Inventory Control at Freescale Semiconductor, Inc." (Winter Simulation Conference 2005). Freescale modelled fabrication-through-final-test in Arena because customer lead time is shorter than supply-chain cycle time, forcing strategic safety stock across the chain; the study links internal on-time delivery, inventory, and WIP changes to customer fill rate. Source: https://informs-sim.org/wsc05papers/212.pdf
- Named public case study, process manufacturing: a discrete-event simulation with OptQuest in Arena for Saba Dimensional Stones Plc (Ethiopia) reports customer service level rising 16.2% (from 58.81% to 70.18%) and inventory cost falling by about 28.8 million Ethiopian Birr. Source: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s13198-025-02997-w
- Method rationale in the literature: "analytical models could not be used to solve dynamics, stochastics and uncertainties problems due to their complexity and non-linearity," so such problems "could be solved more easily using simulation with an optimizing algorithm"; simulation-optimization with data resampling is an active MEIO research line. Sources (search summary) https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s13198-025-02997-w and arXiv 1901.00090 https://arxiv.org/pdf/1901.00090
- Monte Carlo pattern for setting stock: run thousands of scenarios over holding, stockout, and total cost to trace a U-shaped cost curve and read off the optimal level (example: 10,000 scenarios). Source: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2949863526000191
- Vendor tools that embed simulation for stock-setting: LLamasoft/Coupa Supply Chain Guru ("simulate real-world behavior to enable true what-if capabilities") and ToolsGroup's stochastic/combinatoric modelling both sit here. Source: https://www.sdcexec.com/warehousing/news/11185315/llamasoft-inc-breakthrough-in-multiechelon-inventory-optimization

## 6. Willems' 38-supply-chain data set — what and where

- Citation: S.P. Willems, "Data Set — Real-World Multiechelon Supply Chains Used for Inventory Optimization," Manufacturing & Service Operations Management (MSOM), Winter 2008, Vol. 10, No. 1, pp. 19–23, DOI 10.1287/msom.1070.0176. It contains 38 real multi-echelon supply chains implemented in practice, with networks up to about 2,025 stages. Sources: https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/msom.1070.0176 and https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Data-Set-Real-World-Multiechelon-Supply-Chains-Used-Willems/232c451fcf58dbcc1527de6d02cd6e76aea9e871

  - Author's site (older UTK host): http://willems.utk.edu/ (his current site is https://seanwillems.com/, which exposed no direct data link on the pages that render)
  - Google Drive mirror posted by the Bruggen blog: https://drive.google.com/file/d/1jfheHEKIZK1cnkC8vs-M2vQIcFCtEJ1o/view

Per chain there are three tabs: an F-tab (network picture), an SD-tab (stage statistical data), and an LL-tab (stage-to-stage links / graph structure). Source: https://blog.bruggen.com/2020/03/supply-chain-management-with-graphs.html
