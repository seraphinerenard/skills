# Exemplars: real writing to calibrate against

Read this file before long-form work. The excerpts are verbatim from the cited sources (transcription variants flagged where they exist). Steal the moves, never the sentences: what these writers share is a set of behaviours, and the last section names them.

A warning that motivates the whole file: your own defaults are the accumulated average of everyone's writing, which is exactly what these writers refused to produce. When your draft feels smooth and complete, compare it against these and notice what is missing: commitment, mechanism, a specific reader, and the willingness to sound like a person.

## Churchill, "Brevity" memo to the War Cabinet, 9 August 1940

> "To do our work, we all have to read a mass of papers. Nearly all of them are far too long. This wastes time, while energy has to be spent in looking for the essential points. I ask my colleagues and their staffs to see to it that their Reports are shorter."

> "Let us have an end of such phrases as these: 'It is also of importance to bear in mind the following considerations…', or 'Consideration should be given to the possibility of carrying into effect…' Most of these woolly phrases are mere padding, which can be left out altogether, or replaced by a single word. Let us not shrink from using the short, expressive phrase, even if it is conversational."

> "Reports drawn up on the lines I propose may at first seem rough as compared with the flat surface of officialese jargon. But the saving in time will be great, while the discipline of setting out the real points concisely will prove an aid to clearer thinking."

**Steal:** he names the cost (time, energy) before issuing the rule; he quotes the exact bad phrases rather than describing them; he explicitly licenses conversational register; and the close concedes the tradeoff ("may at first seem rough") and commits anyway. Minor transcription variants exist across archives ("an end of such phrases" versus "an end to phrases such as these"). Source: Harvard Kennedy School policy memos collection; policymemos.hks.harvard.edu.

## Orwell, "Politics and the English Language", 1946

The six rules:

> "i. Never use a metaphor, simile or other figure of speech which you are used to seeing in print. ii. Never use a long word where a short one will do. iii. If it is possible to cut a word out, always cut it out. iv. Never use the passive where you can use the active. v. Never use a foreign phrase, a scientific word or a jargon word if you can think of an everyday English equivalent. vi. Break any of these rules sooner than say anything outright barbarous."

The before/after that teaches more than any rulebook. Ecclesiastes:

> "I returned and saw under the sun, that the race is not to the swift, nor the battle to the strong, neither yet bread to the wise, nor yet riches to men of understanding, nor yet favour to men of skill; but time and chance happeneth to them all."

Orwell's parody of the same sentence in modern prose:

> "Objective consideration of contemporary phenomena compels the conclusion that success or failure in competitive activities exhibits no tendency to be commensurate with innate capacity, but that a considerable element of the unpredictable must invariably be taken into account."

**Steal:** put the concrete image back ("bread", "the race", "the battle"), and notice that the parody's crimes are the modern machine tells: nominalization stacks, zero imagery, hedged abstraction, no actor doing anything. Orwell also supplies the diagnosis: modern prose "consists less and less of words chosen for the sake of their meaning, and more and more of phrases tacked together like the sections of a prefabricated hen-house." Source: orwellfoundation.com.

## Buffett, preface to the SEC Plain English Handbook, 1998, and the 2002 shareholder letter

> "One unoriginal but useful tip: Write with a specific person in mind. When writing Berkshire Hathaway's annual report, I pretend that I'm talking to my sisters. I have no trouble picturing them: Though highly intelligent, they are not experts in accounting or finance. They will understand plain English, but jargon may puzzle them. My goal is simply to give them the information I would wish them to supply me if our positions were reversed. To succeed, I don't need to be Shakespeare; I must, though, have a sincere desire to inform."

From the 2002 letter (verified against the published PDF, berkshirehathaway.com/letters/2002pdf.pdf):

> "Charlie and I are of one mind in how we feel about derivatives and the trading activities that go with them: We view them as time bombs, both for the parties that deal in them and the economic system."

> "Or say you want to write a contract speculating on the number of twins to be born in Nebraska in 2020. No problem – at a price, you will easily find an obliging counterparty."

> "In extreme cases, mark-to-model degenerates into what I would call mark-to-myth."

> "When Charlie and I finish reading the long footnotes detailing the derivatives activities of major banks, the only thing we understand is that we don't understand how much risk the institution is running."

**Steal:** the audience-of-one device (a named, pictured reader); total commitment ("time bombs", no hedge); absurdly concrete hypotheticals doing the work abstractions cannot (Nebraska twins); a coined phrase that carries the analysis (mark-to-myth); and admitting ignorance as an argument, which is the opposite of the machine instinct to sound complete.

## Feynman on circular jargon

From the 1966 National Science Teachers Association address "What Is Science?" (feynman.com/science/what-is-science): a physics textbook shows a wind-up toy dog and asks "What makes it move?", answering that energy makes it move. Feynman:

> "It would be equally well to say that 'God makes it move,' or 'spirit makes it move,' or 'movability makes it move.'"

> "Without using the new word which you have just learned, try to rephrase what you have just learned in your own language."

And the alternative he offers: "Take apart the toy; see how it works. See the cleverness of the gears; see the ratchets." The companion bit, from *Surely You're Joking, Mr. Feynman!* ("Judging Books by Their Covers"): substitute a nonsense word and notice nothing changes — "That's the general principle: 'Wakalixes makes it go.' There's no knowledge coming in."

**Steal:** the rephrase test is mechanically applicable to your own drafts. Take any sentence leaning on a vocabulary word ("demand drives price", "the platform ensures scalability") and restate it without the word. If nothing is left, there was nothing there. Explanation means a mechanism you can point at: gears and ratchets.

## Vonnegut, "How to Write with Style", 1980

The eight rule titles: Find a subject you care about. Do not ramble, though. Keep it simple. Have the guts to cut. Sound like yourself. Say what you mean to say. Pity the readers. For really detailed advice (read Strunk and White).

> "'To be or not to be?' asks Shakespeare's Hamlet. The longest word is three letters long."

> "If a sentence, no matter how excellent, does not illuminate your subject in some new and useful way, scratch it out."

> "I myself find that I trust my own writing most, and others seem to trust it most, too, when I sound most like a person from Indianapolis, which is what I am."

**Steal:** simplicity framed as respect for the reader's actual cognitive load, and trust built by sounding like where you are from rather than like prose in general.

## Paul Graham, "Write Like You Talk", 2015

> "Here's a simple trick for getting more people to read what you write: write in spoken language."

> "Is this the way I'd say this if I were talking to a friend? If it isn't, imagine what you would say, and use that instead."

> "Informal language is the athletic clothing of ideas."

> "If you simply manage to write in spoken language, you'll be ahead of 95% of writers. And it's so easy to do: just don't let a sentence through unless it's the way you'd say it to a friend."

**Steal:** the per-sentence gate. It is checkable, one sentence at a time, and it is the same test the parent skill calls the governing test. Source: paulgraham.com/talk.html.

## Zinsser, On Writing Well

> "Clutter is the disease of American writing. We are a society strangling in unnecessary words, circular constructions, pompous frills and meaningless jargon."

> "The secret of good writing is to strip every sentence to its cleanest components."

Zinsser's running villain (paraphrase of the book's famous example): the airline pilot who announces he is "presently anticipating experiencing considerable precipitation" instead of saying it may rain.

**Steal:** the diagnosis-metaphor opening with zero hedging, and the habit of naming a concrete villain instead of lamenting abstractly.

## Strunk, The Elements of Style, 1918

Rule 13, verified against the published text:

> "Vigorous writing is concise. A sentence should contain no unnecessary words, a paragraph no unnecessary sentences, for the same reason that a drawing should have no unnecessary lines and a machine no unnecessary parts. This requires not that the writer make all his sentences short, or that he avoid all detail and treat his subjects only in outline, but that every word tell."

His replacement pairs, verbatim: "the question as to whether → whether"; "used for fuel purposes → used for fuel"; "he is a man who → he"; "in a hasty manner → hastily"; "owing to the fact that → since"; "the fact that he had not succeeded → his failure"; "the fact that I had arrived → my arrival". And: "the expression *the fact that* should be revised out of every sentence in which it occurs."

Rule 12 is the anti-hedging rule, verbatim:

> "Make definite assertions. Avoid tame, colorless, hesitating, non-committal language."

With pairs: "not honest → dishonest"; "did not remember → forgot"; "did not pay any attention to → ignored". And Rule 11's active-voice pair: "There were a great number of dead leaves lying on the ground. → Dead leaves covered the ground."

**Steal:** the carve-out matters as much as the rule: not that all sentences be short, "but that every word tell." Density of meaning, never brevity for its own sake.

## Bezos, 2017 Amazon shareholder letter

> "We don't do PowerPoint (or any other slide-oriented) presentations at Amazon. Instead, we write narratively structured six-page memos. We silently read one at the beginning of each meeting in a kind of 'study hall.'"

> "The great memos are written and re-written, shared with colleagues who are asked to improve the work, set aside for a couple of days, and then edited again with a fresh mind. They simply can't be done in a day or two."

**Steal:** narrative prose as a forcing function for thought (the reason the ideation skill demands a story before a deck), and the plain admission that quality is rewriting.

## SEC Plain English Handbook, 1998: before and after

Verbatim pairs from the handbook (via wilbers.com/sec.htm):

- "The market's advances are often driven by large-capitalization stocks" → "Large-capitalization stocks often drive the market's advances."
- "The volatility of the market should be taken under consideration by short-term investors in the selection and purchase of stocks" → "If you are a short-term investor, you should consider the volatility of the market when you select which stocks to purchase."
- "The Boomer Fund has achieved capital appreciation of your funds" → "The value of your shares in the Boomer Fund has increased."

**Steal:** every rewrite does the same three things: finds the actor, activates the verb, and addresses a person. That is the whole plain-language method, demonstrated by a securities regulator.

## What the exemplars converge on

Five behaviours, each the photographic negative of a machine tell:

1. **Write the sentence you would say aloud** (Graham's gate, Vonnegut's Indianapolis, Churchill's "even if it is conversational"). Negative of: officialese, jargon stacks, performed formality.
2. **Every word tells** (Strunk 13, Orwell's rule iii, Zinsser's clutter). Negative of: filler phrases, -ing trailer clauses, padding triads.
3. **Commit** (Strunk 12, Buffett's time bombs). Negative of: hedging stacks, both-sidesing, "it could be argued".
4. **Mechanism over vocabulary** (Feynman's rephrase test, the SEC's actor-verb rewrites). Negative of: hollow causal claims, copula avoidance, abstraction.
5. **Write to one pictured reader** (Buffett's sisters). Negative of: text optimized for the widest variety of cases, which is the statistical definition of machine style.
