# Algorithmic Pricing Failures and Regulatory Response, 2024-2026: Sourced Brief

All URLs accessed 2026-07-12. Figures reflect the cited source's characterization; where a headline number comes from a journalist's reading of a court document rather than the agency's own text, that is flagged.

## 1. Algorithmic price spirals / feedback loops

The book "The Making of a Fly" by Peter Lawrence reached a listed price of $23,698,655.93 (plus $3.99 shipping) on Amazon in April 2011, driven by two third-party sellers' automated repricers. (https://www.michaeleisen.org/blog/?p=358, accessed 2026-07-12)
The mechanism: seller "profnath" set its price once a day to 0.9983x the price of seller "bordeebook"; "bordeebook" then reset its price to 1.270589x profnath's price, so each daily cycle multiplied the price by roughly 1.269, compounding upward with no ceiling. (https://www.michaeleisen.org/blog/?p=358, accessed 2026-07-12)
The price peaked on April 18, 2011; on April 19 profnath's price dropped to $106.23 and bordeebook followed to $106.23 x 1.27059 = $134.97, ending the spiral. (https://www.michaeleisen.org/blog/?p=358, accessed 2026-07-12)
Neither algorithm read the other's absolute level as implausible because each only referenced the other's price by a fixed ratio, so the loop had no sanity bound. (https://www.theregister.com/2011/04/26/amazon_pricing_algorithms/, accessed 2026-07-12)
Evolutionary biologist Michael Eisen documented the incident; it is the canonical example of a two-agent repricing feedback loop absent a price cap. (https://scholarlykitchen.sspnet.org/2011/04/25/algorithmic-pricing-on-amazon-is-a-book-about-flies-worth-23698655-93-plus-shipping/, accessed 2026-07-12)

## 2. Surge-pricing backlash and regulatory action

### Uber Sydney siege (2014)
On December 15, 2014, during the Lindt Cafe hostage siege in Sydney's Martin Place, Uber's automatic surge pricing pushed the minimum fare for pickups near the siege to A$100 (about US$82), more than four times the pre-event fare. (https://money.cnn.com/2014/12/15/news/uber-sydney-cafe-siege-fares/index.html, accessed 2026-07-12)
Uber initially defended it ("Fares have increased to encourage more drivers to come online & pick up passengers in the area"), then reversed within about an hour, refunded affected riders, and offered free rides out of the area. (https://www.nbcnews.com/storyline/sydney-hostage-standoff/ubers-surge-pricing-near-siege-sydney-sparks-outrage-n268371, accessed 2026-07-12)

### Uber NY AG settlement (2014)
In July 2014, NY Attorney General Eric Schneiderman announced an agreement under which Uber caps prices during "abnormal disruptions of the market" (New York's 1978 price-gouging statute), charging no more than the fourth-highest price it charged in that area over the prior 60 days, and donating its commission on surge fares during emergencies to the American Red Cross. Uber applied the policy nationally. (https://ag.ny.gov/press-release/2014/ag-schneiderman-announces-agreement-uber-cap-pricing-during-emergencies-and, accessed 2026-07-12)

### Wendy's "dynamic pricing" (Feb 2024)
On the February 15, 2024 Q4-2023 earnings call, CEO Kirk Tanner described a roughly $20 million investment in digital menu boards and said Wendy's would in 2025 "begin testing more enhanced features like dynamic pricing and day-part offerings along with AI-enabled menu changes and suggestive selling." (https://www.fool.com/earnings/call-transcripts/2024/02/15/wendys-wen-q4-2023-earnings-call-transcript/, accessed 2026-07-12)
Late-February coverage framed this as "Uber-style surge pricing"; #BoycottWendys trended and Burger King ran a "No urge to surge" promotion. (https://www.axios.com/2024/02/29/wendys-surge-pricing-ai-backlash-internet, accessed 2026-07-12)
On February 27, 2024, Wendy's issued a statement saying the comments were "misconstrued," that it never intended to raise prices at peak demand, and that digital boards would be used to lower prices and run discounts during slow periods. (https://www.npr.org/2024/02/28/1234412431/wendys-dynamic-surge-pricing, accessed 2026-07-12; https://www.restaurantdive.com/news/wendys-backtracks-on-dynamic-pricing-after-consumer-backlash/708799/, accessed 2026-07-12)

## 3. The RealPage case (DOJ + state AGs, private MDL, and city/state bans)

### The DOJ suit
On August 23, 2024, DOJ and the AGs of North Carolina, California, Colorado, Connecticut, Minnesota, Oregon, Tennessee, and Washington (8 states) filed a civil antitrust suit against RealPage Inc. in the Middle District of North Carolina, No. 1:24-CV-00710. (https://www.justice.gov/archives/opa/pr/justice-department-sues-realpage-algorithmic-pricing-scheme-harms-millions-american-renters, accessed 2026-07-12; https://www.naag.org/multistate-case/united-states-and-plaintiff-states-v-real-page-m-d-n-c-124-cv-00710/, accessed 2026-07-12)
Claims: Sherman Act Section 1 (unlawful information-sharing agreement to align rents via a common algorithm) and Section 2 (monopolization / attempted monopolization of the commercial revenue-management software market). (https://www.justice.gov/archives/opa/pr/justice-department-sues-realpage-algorithmic-pricing-scheme-harms-millions-american-renters, accessed 2026-07-12)
Products named: RealPage's revenue-management software, including YieldStar and AI Revenue Management (AIRM). (https://www.multifamilydive.com/news/doj-realpage-antitrust-camden-blackstone-sortland-pinnacle-willow-bridge-/736760/, accessed 2026-07-12)
Market-power allegation: RealPage commands roughly 80% of the U.S. market for commercial revenue-management software for conventional multifamily housing; its software sets rents for more than 3 million apartment units (using AIRM/YieldStar) and draws on competitively sensitive data from over 16 million units. (https://www.mofo.com/resources/insights/240903-doj-rages-against-the-machine-the-feds, accessed 2026-07-12)
Harm example in the complaint: one landlord told RealPage it began raising rents within a week of adopting the software and, within 11 months, had raised them more than 25%. (https://www.washingtonpost.com/business/2024/08/23/justice-department-realpage-lawsuit-rent/, accessed 2026-07-12)

### The hub-and-spoke theory
DOJ's theory: RealPage is the "hub," competing landlords are the "spokes," and the shared algorithm is the coordinating mechanism; landlords feed nonpublic, competitively sensitive data (rents, occupancy, lease terms) into a common tool that returns aligned pricing recommendations, achieving coordination without direct landlord-to-landlord communication. (https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2025/01/the-latest-on-realpage-collusion-by-algorithm-litigation, accessed 2026-07-12)
In the private tenant MDL, the M.D. Tennessee court let the "hub-and-spoke" vertical theory proceed under the rule of reason and did not sustain a per se direct-horizontal-conspiracy theory against the landlords. (https://www.americanbar.org/content/dam/aba/publications/antitrust/magazine/2024/vol-39-issue-1/pricing-algorithms-collusive-outcomes.pdf, accessed 2026-07-12)

### Amended complaint (Jan 2025)
On January 7, 2025, DOJ and (by then) 10 states amended the complaint to add six large landlords as defendants: Greystar; Blackstone's LivCor; Camden Property Trust; Cushman & Wakefield / Pinnacle; Willow Bridge Property Company; and Cortland Management. The six operate more than 1.3 million units in 43 states plus D.C. (https://www.justice.gov/archives/opa/pr/justice-department-sues-six-large-landlords-algorithmic-pricing-scheme-harms-millions, accessed 2026-07-12; https://www.multifamilydive.com/news/doj-realpage-antitrust-camden-blackstone-sortland-pinnacle-willow-bridge-/736760/, accessed 2026-07-12)
Cortland Management was the first to settle (agreeing to stop using competitors' nonpublic data), and its Atlanta headquarters was subject to an FBI raid. (https://www.multifamilydive.com/news/doj-realpage-antitrust-camden-blackstone-sortland-pinnacle-willow-bridge-/736760/, accessed 2026-07-12)

### DOJ settlement with RealPage (Nov 2025)
On November 24, 2025, DOJ announced a proposed settlement with RealPage; it carries no financial penalties and no admission of wrongdoing, and it runs for seven years subject to court approval (Tunney Act; proposed final judgment published in the Federal Register January 21, 2026). (https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/justice-department-requires-realpage-end-sharing-competitively-sensitive-information-and, accessed 2026-07-12; https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/01/21/2026-01009/united-states-of-america-et-al-v-realpage-inc-et-al-proposed-final-judgment-and-competitive-impact, accessed 2026-07-12)
Key terms: RealPage may not use any nonpublic competitively sensitive information (current or historical) in the runtime of any revenue-management product; runtime recommendations may rely only on the subject landlord's own data plus public data; training data must be at least 12 months old (no real-time lease data); reported pricing information may not be more granular than statewide; RealPage may not run market surveys to gather nonpublic intelligence or host meetings where competing landlords discuss nonpublic pricing; and a court-approved monitor gets access to RealPage's code, model-training documentation, and runtime logic. (https://www.mintz.com/insights-center/viewpoints/2191/2025-12-01-last-years-rent-realpage-reaches-settlement-agreement, accessed 2026-07-12; https://www.fenwick.com/insights/publications/dojs-realpage-settlement-a-blueprint-for-safer-algorithmic-pricing, accessed 2026-07-12)

### Private MDL settlements (2025)
In In re RealPage, Inc., Rental Software Antitrust Litigation (No. II), MDL No. 3071, No. 3:23-md-3071 (M.D. Tenn.), plaintiffs reached preliminary settlements totalling $141.8 million with 26 defendants, preliminarily reported around October 1, 2025. (https://www.freshfields.com/en/our-thinking/blogs/a-fresh-take/key-development-in-the-algorithmic-collusion-saga-settling-defendants-to-pay-ove-102lp89, accessed 2026-07-12)
Individual amounts include Equity Residential $56M, Camden Property Trust $53M, and Greystar $50M; the settlement class covers renters who paid rent on a property licensed for RealPage revenue-management solutions from October 18, 2018 through November 21, 2025. (https://openclassactions.com/settlements/realpage-rental-software-antitrust-class-action-settlement.php, accessed 2026-07-12)

### State AG settlement with Greystar (Nov 2025)
On November 19, 2025, a coalition of 9 states (California, Colorado, Connecticut, Illinois, Massachusetts, Minnesota, North Carolina, Oregon, Tennessee) announced a $7 million settlement with Greystar (the largest U.S. property manager); Greystar agreed to stop using or licensing any revenue-management product that uses external nonpublic data to set rents and to stop attending RealPage-hosted meetings of competing landlords. (https://www.ag.state.mn.us/Office/Communications/2025/11/19_Greystar.asp, accessed 2026-07-12; https://oag.ca.gov/news/press-releases/attorney-general-bonta-announces-7-million-settlement-greystar-participating, accessed 2026-07-12)

### City / state bans on algorithmic rent-setting
San Francisco, in July 2024, became the first city to ban algorithmic rent-setting software; Philadelphia followed in October 2024, then Berkeley, and Minneapolis became the fourth city. Bans and similar measures also cover San Diego, Seattle, Providence (RI), Jersey City and Hoboken (NJ). (https://stateline.org/2025/03/28/cities-lead-bans-on-algorithmic-rent-hikes-as-states-lag-behind/, accessed 2026-07-12; https://www.governing.com/urban/minneapolis-becomes-fourth-city-to-ban-rent-price-fixing-software, accessed 2026-07-12)
Philadelphia's ordinance bans software that facilitates landlord "price coordination" by collecting nonpublic pricing, supply, and occupancy data and generating rent recommendations. (https://stateline.org/2025/03/28/cities-lead-bans-on-algorithmic-rent-hikes-as-states-lag-behind/, accessed 2026-07-12)
RealPage sued Berkeley (April 2025) to block its ordinance, arguing it is unconstitutional under the First Amendment; RealPage reportedly fended off the Berkeley ban. (https://www.berkeleyside.org/2025/03/12/berkeley-bans-rent-pricing-software-realpage, accessed 2026-07-12; https://sfstandard.com/2025/07/09/realpage-software-ban-san-francisco-bay-area/, accessed 2026-07-12)
New York State enacted a statute (reported as first-of-its-kind) broadly prohibiting rent-setting software that draws on data from multiple unaffiliated landlords, treating violations as a state antitrust offense whether the inputs are public or nonpublic. (https://www.hoganlovells.com/en/publications/proposed-doj-settlement-provides-guidance-on-use-of-competitive-information, accessed 2026-07-12) [scope/effective date UNVERIFIED against the statute text; sourced to law-firm summary]

## 4. FTC and DOJ attention to algorithmic / surveillance pricing

### FTC 6(b) surveillance-pricing study (July 2024)
On July 23, 2024, the FTC issued 6(b) orders to 8 companies seeking information on "surveillance pricing": Mastercard, Revionics, Bloomreach, JPMorgan Chase, Task Software, PROS, Accenture, and McKinsey & Co. The Commission voted 5-0. (https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/07/ftc-issues-orders-eight-companies-seeking-information-surveillance-pricing, accessed 2026-07-12; https://fortune.com/2024/07/24/ftc-surveillance-pricing-practices-mastercard-jpmorgan/, accessed 2026-07-12)
6(b) authority lets the FTC run studies with no specific law-enforcement purpose; the study targets intermediary firms that use AI plus personal data (location, demographics, credit history, browsing/shopping history) to set individualized prices. (https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/07/ftc-issues-orders-eight-companies-seeking-information-surveillance-pricing, accessed 2026-07-12)

### FTC staff findings (January 2025)
On January 17, 2025, FTC staff released initial findings (a "Staff Perspective") based on documents from Mastercard, Accenture, PROS, Bloomreach, Revionics, and McKinsey & Co. (https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/01/ftc-surveillance-pricing-study-indicates-wide-range-personal-data-used-set-individualized-consumer, accessed 2026-07-12)
Findings: behavioural and demographic signals as granular as a consumer's precise location, mouse movements on a page, and the products left unpurchased in an online cart can be tracked and used to target prices. (https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/01/ftc-surveillance-pricing-study-indicates-wide-range-personal-data-used-set-individualized-consumer, accessed 2026-07-12)
Illustrative example: a seller can confirm from recent purchases that a buyer is a new parent and from recent searches that the baby is ill, then market fast-delivery of a baby thermometer to that buyer at a price premium. (https://www.mccarter.com/insights/ftc-surveillance-pricing-study-finds-personal-data-is-used-to-set-individualized-consumer-prices-and-generate-higher-profits-for-companies/, accessed 2026-07-12)
Note: this was a Biden-era FTC staff release issued in the final days of the Khan chairmanship; it called for continued research and did not itself allege law violations. (https://www.ftc.gov/policy/advocacy-research/tech-at-ftc/2025/01/surveillance-pricing-update-work-ahead, accessed 2026-07-12)

## 5. FTC v. Amazon — "Project Nessie"

The FTC's antitrust suit (FTC and 17 states v. Amazon.com, W.D. Wash., filed September 2023) alleges Amazon ran a secret pricing algorithm internally codenamed "Project Nessie" from roughly 2015 to 2019. (https://www.cnn.com/2023/11/02/tech/jeff-bezos-amazon-ftc-lawsuit, accessed 2026-07-12)
Mechanism: Nessie identified products where Amazon predicted competitors would follow an Amazon price increase; it raised the price, and once rivals matched, Amazon kept the elevated price. Amazon paused it during peak-scrutiny windows (holiday season, Prime Day). (https://techcrunch.com/2023/11/02/unredacted-ftc-suit-shows-project-nessie-price-raising-algorithm-made-amazon-1-4b/, accessed 2026-07-12)
Figures (from unredacted complaint portions, per press reporting): Nessie generated about $1.4 billion for Amazon over its run; between 2016 and 2018 it produced more than $1 billion in excess profit; in 2018 alone Amazon estimated an added $334 million; in April 2018 alone Nessie raised prices on more than 8 million items collectively costing consumers nearly $194 million. (https://techcrunch.com/2023/11/02/unredacted-ftc-suit-shows-project-nessie-price-raising-algorithm-made-amazon-1-4b/, accessed 2026-07-12; https://thehill.com/business/4292178-amazon-made-over-1b-using-secret-algorithm-called-project-nessie-ftc-says/, accessed 2026-07-12)
Caveat: the "$1.4 billion" total is the press's reading of unredacted text; the FTC's public complaint language frames it as extracting "well over $1 billion." (https://www.engadget.com/amazon-ftc-lawsuit-unredacted-documents-project-nessie-secret-price-gouging-algorithm-194800531.html, accessed 2026-07-12)
Amazon's response: spokesman Tim Doyle said Nessie was meant to stop price-matching from driving prices to unsustainable lows, that it ran on a subset of products, did not work as intended, and was scrapped years ago. (https://techcrunch.com/2023/11/02/unredacted-ftc-suit-shows-project-nessie-price-raising-algorithm-made-amazon-1-4b/, accessed 2026-07-12)
Case status: U.S. District Judge John Chun (W.D. Wash.) set a bench trial for October 13, 2026; he let most of the roughly 20 federal and state claims proceed and dismissed the New Jersey, Pennsylvania, Oklahoma, and Maryland state-law claims. (https://news.bloomberglaw.com/antitrust/amazon-poised-for-late-2026-trial-in-ftc-monopoly-power-lawsuit, accessed 2026-07-12; https://www.theregister.com/2024/02/14/amazon_ftc_antitrust/, accessed 2026-07-12)

## 6. Academic work on algorithmic / tacit collusion

### Calvano, Calzolari, Denicolò, Pastorello (2020), AER
"Artificial Intelligence, Algorithmic Pricing, and Collusion," American Economic Review 110(10): 3267-97. (https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Faer.20190623, accessed 2026-07-12)
Core result: simple Q-learning pricing agents in a repeated Bertrand oligopoly consistently learn to charge supracompetitive prices without any communication, sustained by reward-punishment strategies (a finite punishment phase followed by gradual return to cooperation), robust to cost/demand asymmetry, number of firms, and uncertainty. (https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Faer.20190623, accessed 2026-07-12)
Metric: the "collusion index" Δ = (avg profit − Bertrand-Nash profit) / (monopoly profit − Bertrand-Nash profit); Δ = 0 at competitive pricing, Δ = 1 at monopoly. The paper documents substantial Δ (commonly summarized in the 0.7-0.9 range in the baseline two-firm case), with collusion persisting at reported 64% and 56% extra profit above Bertrand-Nash for 3 and 4 firms respectively. (https://fintech.stanford.edu/events/doctoral-seminar/artificial-intelligence-algorithmic-pricing-and-collusion-calvano-et-al-aer, accessed 2026-07-12) [The precise baseline Δ value is summarized differently across secondary sources; treat the 0.7-0.9 baseline figure as approximate and confirm against the AER text before quoting a single number — UNVERIFIED to a single exact value.]

### Assad, Clark, Ershov, Xu — German retail gasoline
"Algorithmic Pricing and Competition: Empirical Evidence from the German Retail Gasoline Market," Journal of Political Economy 132(3): 723-771 (2024); pricing software became widely available in Germany in 2017. (https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/726906, accessed 2026-07-12)
Findings: algorithmic-pricing adoption raised station margins by about 9%, but only in non-monopoly markets; in duopoly markets, margins did not change when only one of two stations adopted, and rose by about 28% when both stations adopted. (https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3682021, accessed 2026-07-12)
Interpretation: the margin gain appears only when competing stations both use the software, consistent with software-enabled coordination rather than pure efficiency. (https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3682021, accessed 2026-07-12)

## 7. Legal / design implications for pricing-algorithm designers

The RealPage settlement is widely read as a de facto compliance blueprint: runtime pricing should use only the user's own data plus public data, never competitors' nonpublic competitively sensitive information routed through a shared vendor. (https://www.fenwick.com/insights/publications/dojs-realpage-settlement-a-blueprint-for-safer-algorithmic-pricing, accessed 2026-07-12)
Specific guardrails firms are advised to adopt: bar nonpublic competitor inputs at runtime; if pooled data trains models, restrict it to aged data (the settlement uses a 12-month-old floor) and coarse geography (no sub-state granularity); avoid vendor-hosted forums where competitors discuss pricing; and ensure genuinely independent price-setting. (https://www.reedsmith.com/our-insights/blogs/viewpoints/102lwqx/algorithmic-pricing-under-pressure-dojs-realpage-settlement-changes-the-rules-f/, accessed 2026-07-12; https://www.hoganlovells.com/en/publications/proposed-doj-settlement-provides-guidance-on-use-of-competitive-information, accessed 2026-07-12)
The core legal exposure for retail pricing design is the "hub-and-spoke" structure: a common software vendor that ingests multiple competitors' nonpublic data and returns aligned recommendations can constitute a Section 1 information-sharing agreement even without direct competitor contact. (https://www.paulweiss.com/insights/client-memos/practical-takeaways-from-the-doj-s-algorithmic-pricing-settlement, accessed 2026-07-12)
Counsel caution that the settlement is not an absolute safe harbour: DOJ under a rule-of-reason theory did not obtain a bright line, and city/state laws (e.g., New York's statute, the municipal bans) go further and can reach even public-data-based tools. (https://www.mintz.com/insights-center/viewpoints/2191/2025-12-01-last-years-rent-realpage-reaches-settlement-agreement, accessed 2026-07-12)
DOJ has separately signalled (statement-of-interest filings and 2026 enforcement commentary) that it views algorithm-facilitated price coordination as a live enforcement priority beyond housing, extending to other sectors. (https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2026/07/collusion-by-code-what-dojs-algorithmic-pricing-enforcement-means-for-the-energy-sector, accessed 2026-07-12)
